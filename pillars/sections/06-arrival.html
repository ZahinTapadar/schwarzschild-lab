Arrival

The engines are quiet. After the long burn and the long braking of the previous section, the ship drifts at walking pace beside the Pillars. The cluster that carved them, NGC 6611, hangs about six light-years away. The tallest pillar is about four light-years from base to tip, so light leaving its foot takes four years to reach its head. Neptune's orbit is 60 astronomical units across, and you could stack about 4,500 Solar Systems along it.

Three pillars, one behind another

From Earth the three pillars look like neighbours standing on the same ground. They are not. In 2014 the MUSE instrument on ESO's Very Large Telescope recorded a full spectrum at every point across them. From those spectra Anna McLeod and her colleagues measured how fast the gas moves toward or away from us, how strongly dust reddens its light, and which faces are lit by the cluster. Together, those clues place the pillars in depth.

Below is that arrangement, built on the same volumetric model of the pillars used earlier in the essay, with the O stars of NGC 6611 as glowing points. It turns slowly on its own. Use the buttons to fly the camera to a viewpoint, or drag to orbit:

Start from Earth's view, then press Side. The pillars are staggered along our line of sight by about a parsec. The small pillar P3 is closest to us. The middle pillar P2 comes next, in front of the cluster. The tall P1 stands behind the cluster, about a parsec farther than P2. Their measured tilts, 40° to 47° out of the plane of the sky, are left out of the model. The orientation of P1's lower part is still uncertain. From the cluster's viewpoint you see what the stars see, the lit caps of all three.

The HUD tracks HD 168076, the cluster's brightest star, a pair of O stars. From Earth it is magnitude 8.2, too faint for the naked eye, with about 3 of those magnitudes lost to dust. From the pillars, about 2 parsecs away, it would shine at magnitude −9.5. That is about 90 times brighter than Venus and 20 times fainter than the full Moon, all in one point. The rest of the cluster adds roughly twice as much again, enough to cast sharp shadows. Most of these stars' power is ultraviolet. To feel as much total power as Earth gets from the Sun, you would have to stop 830 astronomical units from HD 168076, 28 times farther out than Neptune.

Inside the scale

Does a glowing cloud look brighter when you get close? Watch the flight below. It starts at Earth's distance, closes in on the middle pillar, and then flies straight through its head. The HUD in the corner tracks what changes and what does not:

Notice that the picture never gets brighter. The pillars grow from a speck to a wall that fills the sky, and the total light you collect climbs by a factor of about a million, yet every patch of the wall is as bright as it was from Earth. Two inverse squares cancel. The light from any small piece of cloud falls off as the square of your distance. The patch of sky that piece covers also shrinks as the square of your distance. Their ratio, called surface brightness, does not depend on distance at all:

surface brightness = flux ÷ solid angle ∝ (1/d2) ÷ (1/d2) = constant

From Earth the tallest pillar spans 2.6 arcminutes, a twelfth of the Moon's width. From 2 parsecs it spans 36°. That is 840 times wider in each direction, about 700,000 times more sky and 700,000 times more light. Each square degree is unchanged. The same rule is why no telescope can make a nebula brighter than it looks to the naked eye. A telescope makes the image bigger. It cannot raise the light per unit of sky.

There is one honest gain. Between us and M16 lie about 3 magnitudes of dust extinction in visible light, or 2.5 at the red line of hydrogen-alpha. Flying in leaves that dust behind, which is why the surface brightness readout creeps from 20.5 to 18 magnitudes per square arcsecond. That is a factor of 10 for 5,700 light-years of travel.

Then the flight enters the pillar, and the readouts switch to the gas itself. The pillar's interior holds about 130,000 hydrogen molecules per cubic centimetre. Air at sea level holds 2.5 × 1019, two hundred trillion times more. The beam pipes of the Large Hadron Collider hold a few million, so the inside of a pillar is about twenty times emptier than one of the best vacuums engineers keep running at scale. Only the best experimental vacuum chambers, near 10−13 torr, do better. The cavity around the pillars, at about a hundred particles per cubic centimetre, is emptier than almost any vacuum made on Earth.

Watch the "gas passed" readout. Crossing the model's middle pillar, you sweep up about half a gram of gas per square centimetre of windscreen, the same mass as a layer of air 4 metres thick. The measured columns through the real pillars give 2 to 6 metres. At walking pace you would not feel it. You would see it, though. Dust is about one percent of the gas by mass, and such a column dims starlight by 30 to 130 magnitudes. The background stars go out, the glow behind you fades, and in the middle of the pillar you sit in total darkness at about 15 kelvin, where the stars of the "cradle" section are forming.

What your eye would see

So how bright is the glow you were looking at? Eighteen magnitudes per square arcsecond is brighter than a truly dark sky, which is about 21.5 to 22, and roughly as bright as the night sky over a small town. That is around a thousandth of a candela per square metre. At that level your eye changes detectors. Cones, which see colour, give up below about 0.005 candela per square metre, and only the rods are left.

Rods are colour-blind, and their sensitivity peaks at 507 nanometres, in the blue-green. At the 656 nanometres of hydrogen-alpha they are about 2,000 times less sensitive than at the 501 nanometres of [O III]. The red line that dominates every photograph supplies well under one percent of what your rods catch. The scene below shows the same pillars as a long-exposure camera records them and, on the other side of the moving divider, as a dark-adapted eye would see them. Use the buttons to brighten the nebula and watch colour return:

Notice that at the true brightness the eye sees a soft, grey-green haze with darker shapes in it, and the red rims nearly vanish. Colour only starts to return when the nebula is about a hundred times brighter, and the eye's view matches the camera's at about ten thousand times. Observers at the eyepiece report the same: M16 is a grey-green haze around a cluster, and the pillars are dark intrusions that need a 30 centimetre telescope and a good night. Your rods add up light for about a tenth of a second. A camera can add it up for hours.

A pillar made from physics

The repository that hosts this essay also contains the Schwarzschild Lab Observatory. Its Multiwavelength Survey generates a turbulent molecular cloud 6.4 parsecs across, places it 1,740 parsecs away, and lets an ionization front from a young cluster eat into it. The front's speed comes from the balance between recombinations and incoming photons used in Carved by starlight. Where a dense core blocks it, the gas in the core's shadow survives as a pillar pointing at the stars. Each of six instruments then traces rays through the same gas and dust.

It is a statistical synthesis. Nothing in it is a map of M16: its pillars are broader and stubbier, and they have no globules or jets. It shows that the physics in this essay is enough, on its own, to make something you would recognise, and that one dust column explains why Hubble sees silhouettes where Webb sees stars.

Final words

The next time you see this picture, remember how faint its glow really is, about as faint as the dark between the stars, and that a camera waited for hours to gather it for you. If you went there yourself you would find a grey haze, a few blinding stars, and columns dark enough to hide in. The light in the picture left 5,700 years ago, and somewhere inside those cold columns, where no visible light reaches, stars were already gathering mass, and they are still at it.
